Cooking

Ordinary cooking, tighter constraints

Off-grid cooking is home cooking with three limits: one burner or a bed of coals, finite water, and nothing wasted. The reliable answer to all three is the same — one pot, built in layers. Master the base ratios below and shelf staples become dinner without recipes.

At a glance

  • White rice: 2 parts water : 1 part rice, covered 15–18 minutes, rest 10 off heat.

  • Season in ladder order: salt → fat → acid → heat.
  • One pot, one flame: less fuel, less washing-up, fuller flavor.

Cooking without power

Fuel choice, safe placement, carbon-monoxide discipline, and camp-stove practice are covered step by step in Heating & Cooking Safely. Read it before the first flame of any outage: cook outdoors or under serious ventilation, never bring a grill inside, and budget fuel like water. This page assumes those rules and handles the food itself.

The one-pot doctrine

Every extra pot costs fuel to fill, heat, and scrub — and scrubbing costs water you may have carried. So build meals as one vessel on one flame: liquid base first (water, broth from bouillon), then aromatics, then protein, then starch that cooks in the broth and drinks up flavor instead of draining vitamins away with pasta water down a drain. Cleanup is a single pot; leftovers reheat in the same pot; a meal that feeds four tonight becomes tomorrow’s soup with an extra cup of water.

The flavor ladder

  • Salt first — it makes food taste more like itself; almost every “bland pot” is an unsalted pot.
  • Fat second — oil, butter, or a spoonful of nut butter carries fat-soluble flavor and makes scarcity taste like plenty.
  • Acid third — a splash of vinegar, citrus, or canned tomato wakes a flat pot faster than any spice on the shelf.
  • Heat last — pepper flakes or chili, added small and late; heat can always be increased but never removed.

Stretching meals

  • Beans plus rice is a complete protein. Grains lack lysine, beans lack methionine — eaten together across the day they cover each other’s gaps, which is why half the world runs on the pairing.
  • Soup multiplies small things. Scraps of meat, tired vegetables, and bouillon feed six from what would garnish two; thicken with potato, oats, or breadcrumbs if it looks thin.
  • Day-old bread is an ingredient, not waste — fry it crisp in fat, soak it into pudding, crumb it to coat or thicken.
  • Fat stretches calories cheapest: oils carry dense energy per pound when work is heavy and appetites run hot.

One-pot beans and rice

A complete walk-through using only shelf items — the template most one-pot meals follow.

  1. The night before: rinse 1 cup dried beans and soak in 4 cups water. No time? Boil 2 minutes, cover, and rest 1 hour instead.
  2. Drain and refresh: discard the soak water, return beans to the pot with 3 cups fresh water, and simmer partly covered until nearly tender, 45–75 minutes — lowest flame, lid on, saves fuel.
  3. Add the rice layer: 1 cup rice, 2 cups water, a teaspoon of salt, and a spoonful of oil or half a bouillon cube. Stir once.
  4. Cover and cook at minimum heat 15–18 minutes until the water is gone; lifting the lid lets steam escape and ruins the timing.
  5. Rest off heat 10 minutes, then fluff with a fork.
  6. Finish on the ladder: taste for salt, add your acid — vinegar, pickled-pepper brine, or canned tomato — then a pinch of chili if wanted. Feeds 4–6; leftovers fry crisped for breakfast.
If going deeper: campfire cooking zones

Cook over coals, not flame. Flames scorch the outside raw-inside and coat pots in soot; glowing coals radiate even, controllable heat. Burn wood down 30–45 minutes to a coal bed, then rake it into zones: one pile for searing and frying, a single spread-out layer for roasting and simmering, and a bare edge for holding warm. Judge heat by hand count — hold your palm level about five inches above the coals and count seconds held comfortably: 2–3 is hot (searing), 4–5 medium (roasting), 6–8 low (simmering). A Dutch oven bakes with coals beneath plus roughly twice as many on its lid.
