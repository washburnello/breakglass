Growing Basics

Start smaller than your enthusiasm

A packet of seeds costs pocket change and returns weeks of food, but a giant untended first garden teaches discouragement. Three crops grown well beat ten grown hopefully. Everything on this page is ordinary extension-office practice, scaled to a first season.

At a glance

  • Most vegetables need 6–8 hours of direct sun; leafy crops settle for 3–4.
  • About 1 inch of water per week, delivered deep and infrequently.
  • Cool-season crops go in weeks before your last-frost date; beans and zucchini wait until a week after.
  • Compost fixes both sandy and clay soil — the feel-test tells you which problem you have.

Start with sun and soil

Sun decides success more than skill does. On a free weekend morning, check the candidate spot hourly from breakfast to late afternoon and write down where shadow falls. Fruiting crops — tomatoes, peppers, beans, zucchini — insist on 6–8 hours of direct light. Leafy crops such as lettuce and chard produce respectably with 3–4 hours and actually last longer in afternoon shade.

The two-minute soil feel-test

Squeeze a fistful of soil dampened to wrung-sponge wetness:

  • Crumbles apart when you open your hand — loam. Ideal; keep feeding it compost.
  • Gritty, will not hold a shape — sand. Drains fast, dries fast; compost and mulch are your constant companions.
  • Forms a shiny ribbon between thumb and finger — clay. Holds nutrients well but drains slowly and warms late; raised beds and compost help most.

Avoid any spot where water stands in puddles a day after rain. If you garden in an older neighborhood or near a road, spend the few dollars on an extension-office soil test — it checks pH and, importantly, lead before anything edible goes in.

Seeds vs starts

Seeds cost pennies and offer every variety ever bred, but they need time. Starts (nursery seedlings) buy you a month of season and make sense for anything slow to begin: tomatoes, peppers, onions, herbs.

Some crops refuse to transplant gracefully because they resent root disturbance — beans, radishes, zucchini, carrots, and other roots should always be direct-sown where they will grow. The working split: direct-sow beans, squash, roots, and greens; buy starts for tomatoes, peppers, and onions the first year and learn seed-starting later.

Watering rhythm

Vegetables want roughly 1 inch of water per week including rain — about two gallons per square yard. Deliver it as a deep soak once or twice a week, not a daily sprinkle: deep, infrequent watering pulls roots downward and carries plants through a missed week, while frequent light sprinkles breed shallow roots that wilt at the first dry afternoon.

  • Water early morning; evening-wet leaves invite mildew overnight.
  • Aim at the soil, not the foliage.
  • Mulch 2–3 inches of straw, leaves, or grass clippings once plants are up — it roughly halves evaporation and blocks most weeds.
  • The finger test outranks the calendar: if soil is dry at your second knuckle, water; if damp, wait.

Pests without poisons

A poison-free garden starts with accepting cosmetic damage — a hole in a leaf costs you nothing. In order of effectiveness:

  • Hand-pick. Ten minutes a week inspecting leaf undersides catches hornworms, beetles, and egg clusters before populations explode. Drop pests into soapy water.
  • Row cover. Lightweight woven fabric laid over young plants excludes moths and flea beetles entirely. Remove it from squash when flowering begins so pollinators can reach the blooms.
  • Companion basics. Interplant flowers and strong-scented herbs — basil, dill, marigold, nasturtium. They draw pollinators and the wasps and ladybugs that eat aphids, and a mixed planting slows any one pest’s spread across a monoculture bed.

Healthy, compost-fed plants in the right light outgrow most problems. Anything you spray, you eat tomorrow — treat that as the rule.

First-season plan

  1. Map the sun on a weekend day and claim the spot with 6–8 hours, or line up containers on the brightest patio.
  2. Test the soil through the county extension office and work 2–3 inches of compost into the top layer.
  3. Pick three crops from the easiest-wins table above and buy seed plus the minimum tools: trowel, watering can, hose or buckets.
  4. Date it against frost: look up your area’s average last-frost date, set out lettuce and chard about four weeks before, and plant beans and zucchini a week after.
  5. Set the rhythm the day you plant: deep water twice weekly, a ten-minute pest patrol weekly, mulch once plants stand a few inches tall.
  6. Harvest young and often — frequent picking drives beans, chard, and zucchini to produce more — and jot what thrived in a notebook while it is fresh.
If going deeper: container gardening for patios

No yard is not a disqualification. Any spot with six honest hours of sun grows food in containers. Five-gallon buckets with several half-inch drainage holes drilled in the bottom are the workhorse; fill them with bagged potting mix, never garden soil, which compacts and drains badly in pots. Reliable bets: lettuce, chard, and herbs in any pot at least 6 inches deep; a cherry tomato or pepper per bucket; one zucchini in a half barrel. Containers dry far faster than beds — expect to check them daily in summer and feed lightly with compost tea or diluted fertilizer monthly. A wheeled plant caddy lets you chase the sun across a season.
