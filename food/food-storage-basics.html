Food Storage Basics

The philosophy

Store what you eat, eat what you store. A two-week reserve built from your ordinary weekly menu rotates invisibly through your normal cooking — no survival-food mountain gathering dust, nothing exotic to learn to cook under stress.

The four enemies of stored food

  1. Heat — every 18 °F (10 °C) warmer roughly halves shelf life. Cool basements beat warm kitchens.
  2. Oxygen — rancidity and nutrient loss. Unopened cans are sealed; repackaged grains want oxygen absorbers in sealed mylar bags inside buckets.
  3. Moisture — mold and spoilage. Dry storage areas only, off concrete floors.
  4. Pests — mice chew through bags in one night. Glass, metal cans, and hard buckets with tight lids win; bay leaves in grain bins are folklore-adjacent but free.

Rotation discipline (FIFO)

First In, First Out. New cans go to the back, older ones to the front where you grab from. Date-mark anything without a printed date with a marker. One practical habit: cook from the reserve every week and replace what you used — the reserve never ages and nobody learns new recipes mid-crisis.

If going deeper: bulk staples

Rice, wheat berries, dried beans, and oats store 20–30 years when packed dry (under 10% moisture) in mylar-lined five-gallon buckets with oxygen absorbers, sealed hot with a clothing iron. This is a weekend project with real payoff, but start with the rotating pantry above — depth beats breadth.

Nutrition under stress

Emergency appetites run high — cold weather and physical work burn calories fast. Fats and oils carry dense calories in small space; plan roughly double your sedentary oil usage if cooking from scratch. Vitamin needs are met fine for weeks by canned fruit and vegetables.
