Radio Reference

Licensing is the law — and it is easy

FRS radios are license-free for everyone. GMRS requires an FCC license ($35, ten years, one license covers your immediate family, no exam). Amateur radio requires passing written exams. Use only FCC-certified, unmodified equipment on these services, and never transmit on a service you are not licensed for. Listening, on the other hand, is always legal.

FRS & GMRS: the shared channel chart

Nearly every consumer walkie-talkie in the United States shares one channel plan: 22 numbered channels between 462 and 467 MHz. FRS (Family Radio Service) and GMRS (General Mobile Radio Service) overlap on most of them, which means a license-free FRS radio and a licensed GMRS radio can hear each other perfectly well. The differences are power, antennas, and repeaters.

About repeaters

A GMRS repeater listens on a 467 MHz input frequency and instantly rebroadcasts on a 462 MHz output — a +5 MHz offset — stretching a handheld's range across a whole county. Only licensed GMRS stations may use them, and only radios with a duplex/repeater setting can talk to one; bubble-pack FRS radios cannot. FRS never gets repeaters.

Power limits are law, not suggestions

FRS transmits at 2 watts or less anywhere on the band, dropping to half a watt on channels 8–14, and its antennas must stay attached. GMRS runs up to 5 watts on channels 1–7, is banned entirely from channels 8–14, and may reach 50 watts on channels 15–22 with base or mobile stations. Box claims of "36 miles" describe mountaintop-to-mountaintop tests, not streets.

NOAA Weather Radio: the one-way lifeline

NOAA Weather Radio is a nationwide network of more than a thousand transmitters run by the National Weather Service. It broadcasts forecasts, watches, and warnings around the clock — you can only listen. Every weather-band receiver sold in the U.S. tunes these seven frequencies:

SAME alerts wake the radio up

SAME — Specific Area Message Encoding — is the digital preamble that precedes every alert. It names the affected counties by code, so a SAME-capable receiver stays silent until a warning targets your county, then blares a distinctive 1050 Hz attention tone before the spoken message. A dedicated weather radio with SAME programming and battery backup will wake a sleeping household faster than any phone alert that depends on a cell network.

Hand-crank receivers: buy right, test monthly

Choose a crank or solar unit that explicitly lists the NOAA weather band — many emergency radios skip it. Test the crank monthly so charging is not a surprise, keep fresh alkaline batteries as the primary source (cranks charge slowly and age), and confirm the unit also receives AM/FM for news when NOAA transmitters themselves lose power.

Emergency traffic discipline

A radio channel shared by a panicked crowd is worth almost nothing. Discipline is what turns it into a tool, and discipline is mostly restraint:

  • Plain language always. Say "I need help," not codes. Ten-codes vary by agency and mean nothing across groups.
  • Identify station and location first. Name, where you are, status. Everything else can wait.
  • Listen before you transmit. Two stations talking over each other equals zero stations heard. Wait for a pause, then key up.
  • Keep transmissions short. Say it once, clearly, then release the button and listen. Long-winded transmissions block real emergencies.
  • Honor minutes-of-silence windows. Directed nets often hold quiet periods — for example the first five minutes past each hour — when nobody transmits except priority traffic, so weak or distant stations can finally be heard. If a net announces silence windows, respect them absolutely.
  1. Listen on the agreed channel for several seconds — longer if a net is running.
  2. Say who, where, status: "Rivera, corner of 5th and Oak, we are fine and staying put."
  3. Release and wait. Count to five silently before trying again; the other side may need a moment to respond.
  4. Move to a working channel once contact is made, leaving the calling channel clear.

Antenna & reach basics

Range problems are usually antenna problems wearing a power disguise. These frequencies travel almost line-of-sight: whatever your antenna can "see," it can reach.

  • Height beats power. Climbing to higher ground gains far more range than any wattage bump. A radio on a hill hears a valley; a radio in a valley hears itself.
  • Antenna up means range multiplied. Moving a base antenna from the desk to the attic or roofline can double or triple coverage. On handhelds, extend the antenna fully and hold the radio vertical, away from your body.
  • Buildings and terrain kill UHF. The 462/467 MHz band is easily absorbed by concrete, metal walls, and hillsides. Indoors, move upstairs or near a window; outside, get out of the gulch.

Going further: the ham radio starter path

Amateur radio is the only service here that requires study, and that study is why hams run the nets when everything else fails. The entry license is the Technician class: one 35-question multiple-choice exam (26 correct to pass), no Morse code requirement, administered locally by volunteer examiners for a small session fee. The FCC issues the license free, and it renews every ten years at no cost. Study guides from ARRL-affiliated publishers and free online practice exams cover everything; most people pass within a few weekends of casual study.

Why bother: after a real disaster, ARES (Amateur Radio Emergency Service) and RACES (Radio Amateur Civil Emergency Service) volunteers staff health-and-welfare traffic, shelter reporting, and agency liaison nets when phones and internet are gone. Licensed operators can join that work; anyone with a scanner or even an unlicensed receiver can still listen to it. Getting licensed in calm weather means you arrive useful, not curious.
