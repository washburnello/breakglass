Daily Plan Guide, Grades 6–8

How to use this guide

This is a shape, not a script. Middle-school students are moving from guided work toward ownership: the student runs the schedule off this page and the tracker, the parent reviews finished work at a set time each day. Adjust minutes to your student, keep the order predictable, and let the checklist below be printed and pinned up. Works offline; nothing here needs a server.

Core subjects in a steady order; hardest thinking goes first

Split the day if attention fades: two sessions before lunch, the rest after. On light days protect math, reading, and writing first.

The year at a glance

Outcomes to expect by late spring, stated plainly. Pace varies; direction should not.

Literature

  • Finishes whole classic novels unaided and retells plot, setting, and character change from memory
  • Identifies the narrator's point of view and explains how it shades the facts
  • Keeps a reading journal: one quoted line per sitting and a sentence on why it earns its place
  • Compares two characters' choices across books and defends which showed better judgment

Writing & Grammar

  • Writes a tight paragraph on demand: claim, support, landing
  • Builds a five-paragraph essay from a self-made outline over one week
  • Edits own drafts for agreement, punctuation, and sentence variety before anyone else reads them
  • Reaches for the dictionary and a style sheet instead of guessing

Pre-Algebra & Algebra

  • Solves one- and two-step linear equations and proves each answer by substitution
  • Handles fractions, ratios, percents, and negative numbers inside real word problems
  • Graphs points and lines; reads slope as a rate of change
  • Explains why a procedure works, not just how to run it

Earth & Life Science

  • Runs a home investigation with a question, a control, and a dated log
  • Explains water, carbon, and nutrient cycles through compost, rain barrels, and gardens
  • Classifies local plants and animals by structure and habitat in a nature notebook
  • Reads tables and graphs and states what they can and cannot prove

World History

  • Maintains one self-drawn timeline from earliest civilizations to today
  • Tells one event from two viewpoints and explains why the accounts differ
  • Pairs a map with a primary-source excerpt to describe trade, war, or migration
  • Connects a past decision to a consequence visible now

Logic & Study Skills

  • Names common fallacies in ads, arguments, and headlines (list below)
  • Takes outline notes and rebuilds them into a spoken summary without looking
  • Runs a planner: assignments, dates, a weekly review that actually happens
  • Memorizes math facts, vocabulary, and scripture with spaced repetition

Bible & Worldview

  • Studies any passage with observe–interpret–apply and records the findings
  • Places a passage inside a whole book's storyline
  • Answers "why do you believe that?" with a reason, calmly and briefly
  • Grows a recitable list of memorized passages

Materials — austere household edition

  • Printed math and science texts; public-domain readers, grammars, and arithmetic drills print free
  • Bundled classics from the Book Library — read on screen or printed
  • One notebook per subject, or one binder with dividers; pencils, ruler, protractor, graph paper
  • Household lab: measuring cups and spoons, clear jars, funnel, baking soda, vinegar, salt, sugar, oil, yeast; garden soil, seeds, a hand lens
  • A wall calendar or planner page per term; a folder for dated best work

Kitchen-science safety

Any experiment involving flame or heat happens with an adult and follows Heating & Cooking Safely. Never taste a "chemical" result; label every jar; wear eye protection for vinegar reactions. Never mix bleach with vinegar or ammonia — the fumes are poison. Purification demonstrations follow Water Treatment, and hands get washed after soil work per Sanitation & Hygiene.

Assessment without tests

  • Narration: after readings, the student tells it back — orally several times a week, in writing twice. You hear instantly whether it stuck.
  • Self-checked problem sets: answers live in a separate key notebook; missed problems are redone until clean and logged in a mistake journal.
  • Portfolio review: dated best work collects in one folder; at month's end the student presents it and leads the review.

Bible & worldview block

A passage-study method: observe, interpret, apply

  1. Observe. Read the passage twice, slowly. List who acts, what happens, repeated words, hard questions. No opinions yet — just what is on the page.
  2. Interpret. Ask what it meant to its first hearers. Compare another translation, chase a cross-reference, then state the main point in one written sentence.
  3. Apply. Name one concrete thing to know, trust, or do differently this week, and record it. Application stays honest because observation came first.

Twenty minutes covers all three. A parent may guide early in the year; by spring the student should run it alone and bring findings to discussion.

Fallacies worth learning to spot

  • Hasty generalization — one example stretched to prove everything
  • Appeal to popularity — "everyone believes it, so it's true"
  • Attacking the speaker — dodging the argument by insulting its source
  • False dilemma — "either this or that" when other options exist
  • Straw man — restating someone's view weaker so it falls easier
  • Slippery slope — one step must slide to disaster, reason never shown

Weekly practice: find one in an advertisement or headline, name it at the table, and say what a fair version of the claim would look like.
