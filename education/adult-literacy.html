Teaching Adults to Read

Dignity first

Shame is the enemy, not illiteracy. Most adults who cannot read have already survived years of hiding it — signing forms they could not study, memorizing labels, asking for "help" in ways that concealed the problem. They are not slow; adults learn faster than children and bring a lifetime of world knowledge to every lesson. Teach privately, use adult materials always, never baby talk or childish readers, and let your student set the pace. What you are really teaching is that it is safe to be a beginner.

Start with a conversation, not a test

Sit down together somewhere private. Say plainly why you are there and ask what reading would change for them — a license, a label, a form, a verse, a letter from family. Their answer becomes the course map. Then find out gently where the ground lies:

  • Which letters do they recognize by name?
  • Do they know what sounds letters make?
  • Can they read any common words on sight (their own name, STOP, EXIT)?
  • Would they try writing something? A signature, a word, anything at all?

Frame every question as "help me know where to start," never as a quiz. Watch more than you ask. If writing is too exposed for a first meeting, skip it; you will see plenty within a week.

Wherever they start, the ladder above them is climbed the same way. Starting further up just means moving faster through it.

The method ladder

Climb one rung at a time. Do not skip ahead because progress feels good; each rung is load-bearing.

Rung 1: letter sounds — the core thirty

Reading in English runs on about thirty reliable sound-spellings to start: a e i o u b c d f g h j k l m n p r s t v w x y z qu sh ch th ng. One at a time: show it, say its sound, trace it with a finger. Finger-trail cards are easy to make — write the letter large and bold on card stock; if you have sandpaper, glue the shape over it so the finger feels a rough edge. Trace while saying the sound, three times, then write it from memory. Review old letters before adding new ones. Two or three sounds per session is a good pace.

Begin with the letters of the student's own name. A first milestone worth engineering early: writing and then reading their own signature's letters back. Long-vowel spellings, silent letters, and the rest come later — this core unlocks hundreds of words on its own.

Rung 2: blending short words

Once a handful of sounds are solid, blend consonant-vowel-consonant words: c-a-t, m-a-p, h-o-t. Magnetic letters on a baking sheet work; paper tiles cut from a box work just as well. Push the sounds together slowly, then say the whole word fast. Your student already knows these words from life — the discovery that written marks can be sounded out and made to speak is the hinge of everything that follows. Let them experience it many times before moving on.

Order word sets so each reuses known letters: start with cat, hat, map, pan; add pin, sit, ten, bed; then hop, mud, run, sun. Change one letter at a time between neighbors in a set (cat → can → man) so every new word is one move away from a won one. When a blend stalls, drop back a step without comment — stalling is information about the pace, not the person.

Rung 3: first passages — you write them

Commercial beginners' books talk down to adults. Write your own instead, using their vocabulary: their street, their job, their grandchildren's names, the foods they cook. Four to six short sentences on a page, built only from sounds learned so far plus a few taught sight words. Reading a real sentence about one's own life is a different experience from reading "the cat sat."

A sample Rung 3 page, written for one student

Jan gets up at six. She has a job at the mill. Jan packs a ham bun and an apple to eat. Her son Sam walks with her to the bus.

Every word decodable or already banked, every sentence about a life that could be theirs. Write new pages faster than they master old ones; the stack of finished pages becomes visible proof of distance traveled.

Rung 4: the sight-word bank

Some of English's most common words break the rules and must simply be known on sight. Grow a personal bank on index cards, chosen from the student's own goals. Five to ten new words at a time; review the whole bank briefly every session; retire nothing until it has been instant for weeks.

Rung 5: fluency, measured visibly

Pick a passage at their level. Read it once, timing a minute and counting the words managed. Then re-read the same passage across several days, charting each minute-count on a simple paper graph taped where they can see it — three columns across a notebook page will do: date, passage name, words in one minute. The line climbing week over week is proof of progress no encouragement can replace. When gains flatten, move to a harder passage and begin again.

Writing from day one

Writing is reading practiced in reverse, and adults take pride in it quickly. Start with their own name, traced then copied. Move to copywork: short sentences from their sight-word bank and passages, written beneath yours so they can compare shapes. Then begin a diary habit — three sentences a day about anything at all, however spelled. Never grade the spelling in the diary; the point is that written English is theirs to command. Spelling grows through the ladder work.

Practical notes: wide-ruled paper helps far more for adults than narrow college ruling — bigger letters, fewer tangles. Let your student choose print or cursive by what they want to write, not what tradition expects; print serves forms and labels sooner. A hand cramped from decades of non-writing tires fast, so keep writing blocks short and stop before frustration, not after.

Numeracy alongside

Learners who avoid text often feel easier with numbers, so let numeracy open doors. Money counting, clock reading, and measuring go first because adults need them most and succeed soonest; confidence earned there transfers back to reading. The tables in Quick Reference and the tools under Calculators give real material to practice on without embarrassment.

The shape of a session

45 minutes, three to five days a week — frequency beats length
SegmentTimeWhat happens
Review5 minFinger-trace known sounds, read the sight-word bank, one fluency pass
New material10 minOne new sound or two new sight words, traced, said, written
Reading practice15 minYour written passage or graded text, aloud, with help offered only when asked or stalled
Writing10 minName or copywork early on; later the three-sentence diary

Forty-five minutes four times a week beats a single four-hour marathon decisively; the brain consolidates between sessions, and fatigue after forty minutes erodes goodwill fast. End every session on success — a sentence read clean, a word bank conquered — never mid-struggle. For the first two weeks, run shorter: review and one new sound only, ten or fifteen minutes, until sitting down together feels ordinary rather than an event.

Progress and plateaus

Watch for markers rather than dates: sounds recalled instantly without tracing; CVC words blended without pushing tiles; a full sentence read unaided; their name written legibly and unprompted; the fluency chart rising across weeks. Celebrate markers quietly and specifically — adults distrust fanfare but remember exact praise.

Plateaus are normal, usually around weeks four to eight

Nearly every learner stalls somewhere in this stretch: effort continues, numbers refuse to move. It is consolidation, not failure — skills are settling in beneath the surface. Hold steady: keep sessions going, drop difficulty slightly if frustration builds, lean on review and the diary, and wait. Learners who quit during a plateau quit believing, which costs far more than literacy. Say plainly: "This happens to everyone who learns. We keep the schedule; the line moves again."

If a plateau runs past three weeks, change one thing — not everything. Swap the passage topic, teach through a new medium (labels instead of tiles, scripture instead of stories), or let them teach a sound back to you. Movement in any channel reopens the others.

BreakGlass itself as reading material

This site doubles as a practice library, and material with a purpose is remembered:

  • The search bar. Type a banked sight word together, press enter, and read what comes back. Every result is proof that print answers questions.
  • Landing-page tiles. Short labels in clear context — read three aloud at the start of a session as a warm-up.
  • A recipe or how-to page. Read it, then do it. Reading that ends in a cooked meal or a fixed hinge teaches why literacy is worth the work.
  • Hymns and scripture. The Faith & Bible reader holds text many learners already carry in memory; the ear checks what the eye decodes, and many adults find their first fluent paragraphs there.

Print pages freely; pencil-friendly paper beats screens for beginners anyway.
