Daily Plan — 3–5

Grades 3 through 5 about three hours of focused work

How to use this plan

It fits one child or many. Shared subjects (science, geography, Bible) can run across ages at one table while younger siblings follow the K–2 plan; rotate your help through composition and arithmetic one-on-one. Adjust by energy, not age alone: a struggling ten-year-old needs more practice, not humiliation; a fast eight-year-old needs deeper problems, not just more of them.

The daily rhythm

About three hours of core work — split into blocks with movement between them
SubjectMinutesWhat it looks like

Composition30Copywork early in the year, then dictation, then original sentences growing into paragraphs; spelling drawn from their own reading.

Keep the blocks honest by keeping the breaks physical: five minutes of stretching, a chore lap, or walking the property line resets attention better than extra minutes ever will. End arithmetic while it is still going well, not after it has gone sour.

What each subject covers this year

Reading Fluency

  • Read chapter-level books alone for steadily longer stretches.
  • Read aloud smoothly, with expression and few stumble-and-restarts.
  • Retell chapters: main events, motives, and honest predictions.
  • Grow vocabulary by collecting unfamiliar words and actually using them.

Materials: bundled classics from the Book Library plus any books owned or traded; a bookmark; a reading log sheet listing title and pages each day.

Simple assessment: the child reads a page aloud smoothly and summarizes the chapter without help.

Composition

  • Copy, then take dictation of short passages accurately.
  • Write complete sentences that answer a question fully, not barely.
  • By year's end produce a clear paragraph: topic sentence, details, ending line.
  • Spell common words correctly and use capitals and end punctuation without reminders.

Materials: pencils, paper or one cheap composition book, and good sentences to copy pulled from their reading, history, or memory verses — no workbook required.

Simple assessment: the child writes a paragraph on a topic you name, with a beginning, middle, and end, without help.

Arithmetic

  • Addition, subtraction, multiplication, and division facts recalled automatically.
  • Multi-digit addition and subtraction, then confident long multiplication and division.
  • Fractions as parts of a whole and on a number line; decimals through money.
  • One- and two-step word problems; measuring, telling time, making change.

Materials: beans for counters, graph paper to keep columns straight, homemade flashcards, dice and playing cards, kitchen scale and measuring cups, a ruler and tape measure.

Simple assessment: the child solves a two-step money word problem on paper, showing their work, without help.

Science Basics

  • The loop itself: ask a question, predict, test, report.
  • Measure carefully — length, volume, weight, temperature.
  • A dated notebook entry every session: sketch plus two-sentence result.
  • Core ideas met hands-on: plants grow toward light, the water cycle, magnetism, states of matter, simple machines.

Materials: jars, a funnel, magnets, seeds, a thermometer, kitchen supplies, and the same notebook used all year.

Simple assessment: the child sets up a simple comparison test and states what it proved, without help.

Geography & Map Skills

  • Use cardinal directions, map keys, legends, and rough scale.
  • Draw maps from memory: bedroom, route to a neighbor, town, continents and oceans of the world.
  • Trace journeys — family trips, explorers, migrations — on printed or hand-drawn maps.
  • Know hemispheres and the equator; locate places relative to home.

Materials: atlas pages if available, otherwise hand-drawn maps; string for measuring curved distances; a compass if you have one, or the shadow-stick method taught instead.

Simple assessment: the child draws a neighborhood map with a key and north arrow, without help.

Bible & Character

  • Read short passages directly — not only retellings — as skill allows.
  • Memorize one verse a week and keep a growing review pile warm.
  • Discuss a trait of the week with real examples from home life.
  • Begin the habit of a few verses read quietly and personally each day.

Materials: a Bible in any translation — public-domain ones ship in the Faith & Bible reader — and an index-card box for the verse collection.

Simple assessment: the child recites recent verses and explains what a passage meant in their own words.

The week's shape

Four core days, one outdoor day, one life-skills day, one rest day
DayShape
MondayCore day — full rhythm; new arithmetic concept lands here.
TuesdayCore day — full rhythm; dictation replaces copywork once copywork is clean.
WednesdayCore day — full rhythm; science experiment day, since it earns extra time.
ThursdayCore day — full rhythm, plus reciting every verse learned so far this season.
FridayOutdoor day — morning reading and one math game only, then a field walk with map sketching, measuring, and nature notebook entries.
SaturdayLife-skills day — cook doubling a recipe (fractions), write real letters and lists, price out a small shopping trip without a calculator, sort and repair tools.
SundayRest — worship and family, free reading, no formal lessons.

Scripture memory & character

Keep a one-verse-a-week cadence: introduce Monday at breakfast, recite at the start of school daily, solo recitation Friday, casual recall of old verses at dinner. At these grades children can also write the verse from dictation Friday — memory and handwriting in one stroke. Give each trait a household job so it is practiced, not merely discussed.

When the day goes sideways

Sick child, broken routine, long errand: keep Reading Fluency and the Bible passage, and count the day done. At these grades a student can also run the checklist themselves on days you cannot sit with them — that is diligence practiced, not school abandoned.
