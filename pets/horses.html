Horses

Reality check first: a horse is a lifestyle, not a pet

A horse eats and drinks every single day, twice a day minimum, for 25–30 years — no vacations unless you pay someone to cover. Costs run from hundreds to well over a thousand dollars monthly depending on region and arrangement. Read the numbers below honestly before falling in love; the horses are fine without you, but you will not be fine with them unprepared.

At a glance

  • Forage at 1.5–2% of body weight daily — 15–20 lb of hay for a 1,000 lb horse
  • Farrier every 6–8 weeks, forever, trimmed or shod alike
  • Colic = walk the horse and call the vet. Remove feed first; never medicate over symptoms
  • 5–10 gallons of water daily, more in heat and hard work

Reality check

Beyond money, count hours: feeding, water scrubbing (buckets freeze or grow slime), manure management measured in wheelbarrows per week, turnout, grooming, and farrier-day scheduling. Horses do not take days off, and neither does their care. The first big decision is where they live:

Ground manners

Ninety percent of horse life happens beside the horse, not on it. Ground manners are safety infrastructure:

  1. Leading: walk at the horse's left shoulder, rope slack in one hand never wrapped around any body part. Practice halt ("whoa") and backing until the horse stops when your feet stop — in wind, past gates, around tractors.
  2. Tying: tie only to solid objects at wither height with a quick-release knot or breakaway strap, never to fence wire or anything movable. A horse that panics while tied learns whatever it learns in the next ten seconds; teach standing tied calmly in short doses instead.
  3. Feet handling: run a hand down each leg daily, squeeze gently above the fetlock, and reward the foot lifting into your hand. Hold each foot ten seconds, pick it out, set it down. A horse that gives all four feet politely to anyone who asks is a farrier's favorite and an owner's blessing.

First rides progression

  1. Groundwork first: lunge-line work at walk and trot on voice cues teaches the language every ride depends on — and burns the fresh off any young or idle horse before a human gets aboard.
  2. Saddle desensitization: blanket, then pad, then saddle — rubbed over the body first, cinched loosely, tightened gradually while the horse walks. Bucking is usually girth discomfort or surprise, both preventable here.
  3. Bit and bridle acceptance, ideally ground-driving so rein cues are understood before feet leave the arena floor.
  4. First mount in a round pen or small enclosed area: sit, stand still, dismount after a minute. Repeat across days before asking for a walk. Short, boring, successful — that is the formula.
  5. Build duration and scenery slowly: walk-only rides, then a calm companion horse on trail rides, adding trot work last.

The honest recommendation: hire a professional

"Green plus green equals black and blue" — an inexperienced rider training an untrained horse is the classic accident recipe in this sport, and horses are too big to learn on. Paying a reputable trainer for the first months of rides is cheaper than one ambulance ride and produces a horse worth owning afterward. There is no shame in it; there is a lot of orthopedics otherwise.

Health emergencies

Recognize fast, act right, call the vet
EmergencySignsWhat you do
Colic (abdominal pain)Pawing, looking or biting at the flank, lying down repeatedly, rolling, not eating, no manureRemove all feed, hand-walk the horse calmly, and call the vet. Do not medicate — painkillers mask surgical signs. Walk in short sets if the horse rests quietly between.
LamenessHead-bobbing at the trot, heat or throbbing pulse in a hoof, reluctance to move, standing pointing a legRest in a small pen, note which leg and when it shows, feel for heat, and call the vet if it persists beyond a day or the horse will not bear weight.
Not drinkingDry tacky gums, pinched skin staying tented on the neck, thick manure, impaction risk rising hourlyOffer fresh and lukewarm water, add salt, monitor intake by marking buckets, and call the vet if intake stays low — dehydration feeds colic.

Keep your vet's number posted at the barn and know your normal: gum color, gut sounds on both flanks, digital pulses, manure output. Baselines you practiced in calm times are what make emergencies readable.

Tack basics glossary
  • Halter & lead rope — the head collar and attached rope used for leading and tying; the horse's steering wheel on the ground.
  • Saddle pad — the cushion layer between saddle and back; straighten it up into the gullet so it never drags on the spine.
  • Girth (English) / cinch (Western) — the wide strap holding the saddle on; tighten gradually and check again five minutes into a ride.
  • Snaffle bit — the standard gentle mouthpiece of a direct-rein bridle; reins attach and signal one side at a time.
  • Lunge line — a long line for exercising and teaching the horse in circles around you, the groundwork tool behind step one above.
  • Hoof pick — the cheap steel hook that clears stones and packed manure from feet daily; the highest return-per-dollar item in the barn.
