Daily Care Basics

Why daily care is preparedness

An animal in good condition going into a disruption comes out the other side fine. The habits below cost minutes a day and prevent most emergencies — weight on ribs you can feel but not see, clean water always, and knowing what "normal" looks like for YOUR animal.

Water needs

Rough daily water — heat and work increase these a lot
AnimalDaily water
DogAbout 1 oz per pound of body weight (60 lb dog ≈ ½ gallon)
Cat3.5–4.5 oz per 5 lb body weight; wet food reduces drinking
ChickenAbout 1 cup; laying hens in summer drink double
Goat/sheep½–1 gallon; lactating does much more
Horse5–10 gallons minimum

Sizing your stored water: add the animals' daily total to the household's and round up. Water dishes go in shade, scrubbed often — green slime turns animals off drinking exactly when they shouldn't ration themselves.

Feeding through scarcity

  • Know each animal's maintenance ration by weight — measure it once with a real cup so eyeballing works later.
  • Sudden food switches cause diarrhea worse than mild hunger; blend old and new food across a week when substitutes become necessary.
  • Dogs handle variety well; cats do NOT voluntarily eat enough non-meat food — cat food reserves are non-negotiable.
  • Never feed cooked bones, onion/garlic, chocolate, grapes/raisins, xylitol anything, or moldy anything.

Shelter and comfort

  • Dogs and cats tolerate the same indoor temperature band you do; if you're cold, they are — bedding off floors, denser in winter.
  • Outdoor hutches/coops: dry, draft-free (not sealed — airflow without windchill), deep litter for burrowing.
  • Heat kills faster than cold for brachycephalic dogs (pugs, bulldogs), overweight animals, and rabbits. Shade, ventilation, cool water, frozen bottles to lean against.

Watching for trouble

You know your animal's normal. Escalate to a vet (or the medical judgment of an experienced keeper) for: not eating beyond a day (cats — half a day), repeated vomiting/diarrhea, labored breathing, bloated tight belly especially large deep-chested dogs, sudden lethargy, straining to urinate particularly male cats — genuine emergencies all.

Training that pays off in crises

  1. Come when called, rewarded generously — the skill that finds escaped animals.
  2. Crate/carrier = good place, fed there sometimes — evacuation goes from battle to routine.
  3. Handle paws, ears, mouth weekly — injured animals tolerate treatment from hands they trust.
