Raising Chickens

The most practical home livestock

Chicks ordered in spring lay their first eggs around month six, eat kitchen scraps and grain, and fit a surprisingly small footprint. Hens are quiet — it is roosters that crow, and most backyards never need one.

At a glance

  • Start brooder heat at 95°F, dropping about 5°F per week until feathered
  • One nesting box per 3–4 hens — they will queue for a favorite anyway
  • A good layer produces roughly 250 eggs a year, pausing during winter molts
  • Hens lay best with 14+ hours of light; expect fewer eggs November through February without supplemental light

Why chickens first

Compared with goats, pigs, or bees, chickens forgive beginner mistakes: they cost little to start, give a daily product you can inspect, and signal problems visibly while there is still time to react. A small flock of three to six hens supplies a household with eggs across most of the year and turns garden and kitchen waste into fertilizer.

  • Eggs in about six months from day-old chicks — pullets (started hens near laying age) cost more and skip the wait.
  • Hens are quiet. Egg-song is brief and comparable to a dog barking; a rooster crows at dawn and all day. You do not need a rooster for eggs — only for fertile ones.
  • Flock size: three hens is the practical minimum (they are social); six covers a family's eggs with room for a bad week.

Check local ordinances before buying anything

Many cities allow hens but ban roosters outright, cap flock numbers, or require permits and setback distances from property lines. HOAs often prohibit poultry entirely. One phone call to city hall or the county extension office prevents the heartbreaking return trip.

Brooding chicks

Chicks need four things: warmth, feed, water, and a draft-free box. Set the brooder up a day before the chicks arrive.

  1. Build the brooder: a large plastic tote, stock tank, or cardboard appliance box lined with 1–2 inches of pine shavings (never cedar — its oils harm chicks). It needs a secure cover against cats and drafts.
  2. Hang the heat lamp at one end so chicks can move between warm and cool zones. Start at 95°F measured at chick level, and raise the lamp to drop about 5°F each week. By six weeks, feathered chicks handle normal spring temperatures.
  3. Watch the chicks, not just the thermometer: huddling under the lamp means too cold; pressed to the far wall panting means too hot; even wandering means just right.
  4. Set a shallow waterer with clean marbles or pebbles in the tray so day-old chicks cannot drown, and dip each beak once on arrival.
  5. Fill the feeder with chick starter (18–20% protein medicated or non-medicated crumbles — either is sound practice) and keep it full; chicks eat within hours of hatching.
  6. Scoop the waterer daily and change wet bedding promptly — damp brooders breed the diseases that kill chicks. Watch each chick's rear end for the pasty butt problem below.

Coop math

Crowded coops turn mean and sick fast. Size before you buy — the numbers below are standard extension practice.

Space per bird — bigger always works better
SpaceStandard/heavy breedsLight breeds & bantams
Inside the coop (sleeping)4 sq ft per bird2–3 sq ft per bird
Run (outdoor exercise)10 sq ft per bird8 sq ft per bird
Nesting boxesOne box per 3–4 hens, lined with straw or shavings
Roost bar8–12 inches of perch length per bird, higher than the boxes

Four hens therefore want roughly a 2×4 ft coop attached to a 2×8 ft run at bare minimum. Build the door tall enough to walk in — you will clean it weekly. Predator-proofing matters more than cosmetics: hardware cloth (not chicken wire) on every opening, a skirt or buried apron against diggers, and a latch a raccoon cannot slide open.

Do not switch to layer feed early — extra calcium damages young kidneys. Store feed in a metal bin mice cannot chew and buy only what the flock eats in a month.

First eggs arrive around 5–6 months — small, sometimes soft-shelled, occasionally laid from the roost bar. Everything smooths out within a few weeks. Production peaks in years one and two, then drops roughly 10–20% annually; hens still earn their keep as pest control and compost makers long after peak laying. Winter daylight, not cold, is what slows laying — a low-watt coop light on a timer keeps production steadier if local rules allow lights.

Health watch list

  • Pasty butt (chicks): droppings sticking to the vent can block a chick fatally within a day. Check rear ends daily in the brooder; wipe clean with a warm damp cloth, dry thoroughly, dab a little oil, and recheck. Recurring cases suggest chill or dirty water.
  • Mites and lice: check the vent area at night for crawling specks and crusty leg scales. Dust-bathing birds usually manage their own pests; heavy infestations mean treating birds and coop together and repeating in a week to catch hatching eggs.
  • Lethargy: a hen fluffed up, isolating, or not eating is an emergency in progress. Move her somewhere warm with water and feed, and contact a vet or experienced keeper the same day — birds hide illness until they are nearly out of time.

A basic kit covers most of this: petroleum jelly, poultry dust approved for mites, electrolytes, and a small quarantine cage. Handle birds regularly in good times so catching a sick one is routine, not a rodeo.

Beyond eggs: processing and the compost loop

Dual-purpose and meat breeds raised for the table are typically processed between 8 and 12 weeks for tender fryers, with older birds going to slow-cooking. County extension offices publish step-by-step humane slaughter and dressing guides, and some run hands-on workshops — take one before attempting it alone. On the other end of the cycle, soiled coop bedding is garden gold: composted hot for several weeks (turned, kept moist, never applied raw) it becomes safe, finished compost for beds. The flock, the compost pile, and the garden close each other's loops.
